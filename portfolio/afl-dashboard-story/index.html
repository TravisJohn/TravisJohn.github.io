The brief was open on purpose: pretend you work for the AFL’s management team; here’s public match and player data. Build a dashboard for a senior audience, find three key findings, suggest one or two areas to explore.

And be ready to explain three things: how you joined the files, how you know the numbers are right, and what the limits are.

That last line is the real test. It maps straight onto the role: “validating through technical and business reconciliation”, and “Tableau is a must”. So I treated accuracy as a deliverable, not an afterthought.

The brief allowed exactly two files, describing the same games at two very different levels of detail.

games.csv: one row per match, 2,879 rows × 22 columns. Read a row like a scoreboard: the 2019 Grand Final, Richmond 17.12 = 17 × 6 + 12 = 114; GWS 3.7 = 25; 100,014 at the MCG.

stats.csv: one row per player per match, 128,938 rows × 31 columns (e.g. Tim Taranto, 30 disposals; Heath Shaw, 29).

The key is GameId. But one game is 1 row in games and roughly 44 rows in stats: 22 players a side. Join the obvious way and every crowd is copied onto all 44 player rows. The 2019 Grand Final suddenly has about 4.4 million people at it, and every attendance total is inflated about 44×.

That was danger number one, and it shaped how I connected things in Tableau later.

Before building anything I profiled both files: row counts, nulls, duplicates, value lists, and whether the two files agreed with each other. Six traps came out.

  • 1 – Text crowds. Attendance is "100,014", a word with a comma. Tableau can’t sum it until it’s converted.
  • 2 – Empty stadiums. 66 games have attendance 0, every one in 2020 or 2021 (e.g. 2020R0101, Richmond v Carlton at a locked-out MCG). Zero here means not recorded. Counting them would drop the 2020 average from about 7.8K to 6.4K, so I treated them as blanks.
  • 3 – 2024 calendar slip. The AFL added a 4-game Opening Round. games.csv labelled 13 games as Opening Round and reused GameIds: 2024OR01 is Brisbane v Carlton on 8 March and Carlton v Richmond on 14 March. stats.csv followed the real fixture, so joining on GameId alone mis-pairs the whole 2024 season: 207 games needed repairing.
  • 4 – Duplicates. Some 2024 opening games were loaded twice into stats.csv, identical to the last column: 138 rows.
  • 5 – Vanishing zero. Goals.behinds saved as a number: 14.10 became 14.1. 2012R0203, Sydney 14.1 but 94 points; 14.1 would be 85. 651 full-time scores affected; quarter scores have the same problem with no total to check against.
  • 6 – The game that wouldn’t end. 2017 Elimination Final: 8.12 (60) v 9.6 (60), a draw, yet the final is 76–78. Nothing in the file says extra time. This one wasn’t found by looking. It was found by a failing test, two slides from now.

I could have patched all of this inside Tableau with calculated fields. I chose not to. The role asks for someone who validates and reconciles, and the first question anyone asks is “how do you know?” So I rebuilt the data in dbt, where every fix is SQL in its own step, tested and documented.

  • stg_games: crowds become numbers; the COVID zeros become blanks.
  • stg_stats: duplicates dropped, 128,938 rows down to 128,800.
  • int_game_id_map: 2024 games re-matched by season, teams and the order they met.
  • int_quarter_scores: lost zeros rebuilt from the totals, which are always right.

Four tables at clear grains: fct_games (one row per game; attendance lives only here), fct_team_games (5,758 team results), fct_player_games (128,800), agg_player_seasons (9,307). The modelled Grand Final row now carries season_phase, day_of_week, time_slot, matchup, winner and winning_margin (Richmond by 89), everything Tableau needs. Documented in a README.

The test: for every team in every match, player goals must equal scoreboard goals. It failed for one game.
Players had kicked more goals than the full-time score showed. The file never mentions extra time. Fix: a went_to_extra_time flag, and all 28 go green.

The tests are the proof. 28 automated checks run every time the pipeline builds. Two matter most:

  • Nothing lost, nothing added. Total attendance after the pipeline equals the raw file exactly: 93,365,899 people across 2,879 games.
  • The two files must agree. For every team in every match, goals credited to players in stats.csv must equal goals on the scoreboard in games.csv. If the 2024 repair had paired the wrong game, this would fail.

And one day it did fail, for exactly one game: the 2017 Elimination Final, Port Adelaide v West Coast. Full time 8.12 (60) to 9.6 (60), a draw, yet the final score is 76–78. It went to extra time and West Coast won by 2. Nothing in the file says so.

The test didn’t just confirm the data, it discovered something the file never mentioned. I added a went_to_extra_time flag and the test passed. That’s the whole argument for testing: it finds what you didn’t know to look for.

With trusted tables, the question changed from “is this right?” to “what would AFL management actually care about?” I explored attendance, venues, time slots, matchups and match stats, and kept the three findings that held up and pointed to a decision.

Finding 1. The COVID collapse is the obvious headline: 7.8K a game in 2020. The real story is the recovery. Since 2023 the average crowd has sat around 38K a game (38.2K in 2025), above the 2019 level of 36.3K and above every season before COVID.

Honest handling: 2020 is the average of only the 133 games that had a recorded crowd, not a zero. Counting the zeros would have shown about 6.4K.

Finding 2: the fixture shapes the crowd. When and where a game is played matters as much as who plays.

  • Thursday and Friday night games draw around 40% more than weekend day games.
  • The MCG hosts about 1 in 4 games but draws 38% of all attendance.
  • Traditional Victorian rivalries draw 60–80K, about double the league average. Collingwood v Essendon tops the list at 79.8K.

Why it matters: these are levers management actually controls through the fixture. Caveat, which I’ll come back to: the MCG partly “wins” because it’s big. Without capacity data I can’t separate demand from stadium size.

Finding 3. I compared each team’s stats with its opponent’s in every match (that’s what fct_team_games, 5,758 team results, is for), then asked: when a team wins a stat, how often does it win the game?

  • Inside 50s: 72.1%
  • Contested possessions: 69.8%
  • Clearances: 62.3%
  • Tackles: 55.7%

Winning the inside-50 count is the strongest signal of a win; tackling more barely beats a coin toss. Important: this is correlation, not causation. Winning inside 50s goes with winning games; it doesn’t prove one causes the other.

Sketch first. Before opening Tableau I mocked the dashboard in HTML, using only chart types Tableau can draw and figures from the same tables. The mock became a build checklist: each sheet’s rows, columns, marks, filters, and the exact number it should show.

1 – Relationships, not joins. fct_games is the base table; fct_team_games relates on game_id; the player-season table is its own data source. Relationships keep each measure at its own level of detail, so attendance is never copied across player rows. The check: total attendance in Tableau read 93,365,899 across 2,879 games, exactly the raw file. One number proves the connection.

Dashboards. Crowds: four KPI tiles; season trend with a grey COVID band; day × time-slot heatmap; top 10 matchups; venue share of crowds (bar) vs share of games (dot). Performance: win rate by stat, team form heatmap (orange poor, blue strong), player consistency scatter. Plus an About the Data page: sources, fixes, limitations.

2 – The top-10 bug. Adding colour to highlight Collingwood made the chart show far more than 10 bars. The ranking is a table calculation; the colour field made it restart within each colour group. Two groups, two top 10s.

3 – Cross-dashboard action. Hover a team on the form heatmap: “See crowds for Collingwood”. Click and Crowds opens filtered. Tested with a known answer: 326 games, and the tile read 326. Two lessons: the Team quick filter still says “(All)”, because Tableau doesn’t update a quick filter when an action filters the same field; and league-wide headlines contradicted a team view (“MCG draws 38%” over a bar reading 84% for Collingwood). Fix: one subtitle line saying headlines describe all teams.

4 – Shipping. Cleared filters so the tile read 2,879 again; Hide All Sheets left three tabs (Crowds, Performance, About the Data); saved Travis_Villanueva_AFL_Assessment.twbx with data inside; published to my Tableau Public profile alongside 38 earlier vizzes; used the profile link (always latest, has Download) rather than the Share snapshot; emailed the hiring team.

Honest limits

What the data can’t say, and where I’d dig next.

Limits

Stadium size drives crowd size

Without capacity data, the MCG always “wins”.

Correlation, not causation

Winning inside 50s goes with winning; it doesn’t prove cause.

2020–21 aren’t normal seasons

Crowds restricted; 2020 quarters shortened. Flagged, not mixed in.

“Home team” is a label

At shared grounds like the MCG, it’s a fixture label, not true home advantage.

Two areas to explore

Explore 1

Fill rate, not crowd size

Add stadium capacity to measure how full a venue is, not how big.

Explore 2

Move the big matchups to Thu/Fri nights?

Test whether it lifts total season attendance.

Honesty about limits is part of the story, and part of the brief.

  • Crowd size is driven by stadium size. Without capacity data, the MCG always “wins”. That’s why fill rate is the first area to explore.
  • Findings are correlations. Winning inside 50s goes with winning games; it doesn’t prove one causes the other.
  • 2020 and 2021 aren’t comparable to normal seasons: crowds were restricted and 2020 quarters were shortened. COVID zeros treated as blanks; seasons flagged rather than mixed in.
  • “Home team” at shared grounds like the MCG is a fixture label, not a true home advantage.

Explore: (1) stadium capacity data, to measure how full a venue is rather than how big; (2) whether moving high-drawing matchups into Thursday and Friday night slots lifts total season attendance, which combines Finding 2’s two levers.

The assessment was about football, but it was really a rehearsal of the job. Almost every line of the role description shows up somewhere in the build (slide numbers in the right column).

The three questions, in one line each:

  • Join: related on game_id in Tableau rather than joined, after repairing the 2024 IDs in dbt, so attendance is never multiplied by player rows.
  • Accuracy: total attendance reconciles to the raw file at 93,365,899, and a cross-file test ties every player’s goals to the scoreboard.
  • Limits: stadium capacity, correlation not causation, and COVID seasons flagged rather than mixed in.

Bridge to health insurance: swap “games and players” for “members and claims” and the discipline is identical: profile, model at the right grain, reconcile, test, then tell the story to the people making decisions. (Framing line only; not from the assessment data.)
