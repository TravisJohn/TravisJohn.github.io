Too Good to Be True: Hunting for Data Leakage

There's a moment every data person knows. You train a model, check the score, and it's far better than you expected. The first reaction is a little thrill. With experience, the second reaction gets louder: what did I break?

Most of the time the answer is data leakage. Information has crept into your training data that you wouldn't actually have when the model makes a real prediction. The model isn't clever. It's peeking at the answers.

What leakage looks like

The textbook cases are easy to spot. The sneaky ones hide in features that look perfectly reasonable:

  • A column that gets filled in after the event you're predicting, like a status or outcome flag that's updated later.
  • Aggregates built over the whole dataset, future included, such as a "season average" that already counts games yet to be played.
  • Rows from the same player, customer or event landing in both training and test, so the model memorises instead of generalising.
  • Scaling or imputation fitted on all the data before the split.

🏀 An example from NoFlightZone

On my NBA analytics channel, NoFlightZone, this trap is everywhere. Say you want to predict whether a team wins. Feed in full-game box-score stats like points, plus/minus or field goals made, and the model looks amazing. Of course it does, because those numbers are basically the final score in disguise. You'd never have them before tip-off.

The honest version only uses what you'd know beforehand: form up to the previous game, rest days, home or away, and who's available. The score drops, sometimes a lot, but it's a real score.

I fell into this one myself. I was building an L1 (lasso) regression on Spurs game data to find which features actually drive a win. I knew points were off limits, so I took the scores out and felt pretty pleased with myself. The model still looked brilliant. Then I spotted it: I'd left in plus/minus. Plus/minus tracks the score difference while a player is on court, so added up across the team it's the final margin. The model hadn't found what wins games. It had found the scoreboard. Once I took it out, the model got noticeably weaker, but now it was actually telling me something.

My leakage checklist

  1. The timestamp question. For every feature, ask whether you'd actually know that value at the exact moment of prediction.
  2. Split by time. Train on the past and test on the future. A random split can quietly hand the model tomorrow's information.
  3. Watch feature importance. When one feature carries the whole model, treat it as a suspect, not a hero.
  4. Fit inside the split. Scalers, encoders and imputers should learn from training data only.
  5. Bug first, breakthrough second. If a simple model easily beats the experts or the baseline, assume something's wrong until you've proved otherwise.

🤖 It happens with LLMs too

This links back to yesterday's post about writing the test before the prompt. If examples from your golden eval set also turn up in your prompt's few-shot examples, the prompt "passes" because it has already seen the answers. Keep your eval set apart from anything the model is shown, just like an untouched holdout set.

🧾 The accountant's instinct

Back in my finance analyst days, reconciling eggs against planning reports at San Miguel Purefoods, a reconciliation that balanced perfectly on the first try never got a celebration. It got a second check. Real data is messy, and a result with no mess in it deserves a second look.

If I had to explain this to a stakeholder, I'd keep it simple. The first score was like a student who'd seen the exam answers: impressive in the room, useless on the job. The new score is lower because it only uses what we'd really know before the game, which makes it the number we can trust when it counts. I'd much rather promise a modest result and deliver it than show off a great one that falls over the first time it meets real data.

A smaller, honest number beats an impressive one that falls over in production, every time.
